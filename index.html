Twelve hours after: how does a speech steady a nation?

George W. Bush addressed the American public from the Oval Office at 8.30 p.m. on 11 September 2001. You will read it twice: once for what it does to you, once for how it does it.

1 The triad

Ethos

character
  • confidence, experience, knowledge
  • credentials, endorsements
  • morals
Why should I trust you?

Logos

logic
  • references, data, statistics
  • case studies, research, analysis
  • analogies
Does it add up?

Pathos

emotion
  • images, stories
  • emotive language, personal feelings
  • inspirational quotations
How should I feel?

These are ways of persuading, not boxes for words: one sentence can work in more than one way. Keep three questions apart. Appeal: how is it trying to persuade? Device: which technique is doing it? Effect: what might it make the audience think, feel or accept?

3 Who is “we”? Who is left out?

The speech does real work: it consoles and steadies a frightened audience. The question for this step is what else its language does while it does that. Switch the lens on in the top bar, read the counts, then pick one question per pair.

A count shows where to look, not what it means. Does the speech merely describe an American community, or does it help to construct one?

Context: what the speech could not yet know
At 8.30 p.m. on 11 September 2001 the attacks were about twelve hours old, and this speech names no group and no country. On the night its vocabulary is still partly that of crime and justice (“bring them to justice”), alongside the vocabulary of war (“the war against terrorism”). Nine days later, on 20 September, Bush addressed Congress and named al-Qaeda and the Taliban; US-led air strikes on Afghanistan began on 7 October. Keep two questions apart: what does this speech say and do on the night, and what did later events do with its language? The second is worth asking, but it is hindsight. Do not judge the speech by what its author could not yet know.
The nameless enemy
The speech never names the attackers, a group, a country or a grievance. What does the word “evil” do that a name could not? What does it make unnecessary: explanation, history, negotiation?
Beacon and light
“The brightest beacon for freedom… no one will keep that light from shining.” Light and darkness have long been the vocabulary of empire: enlightening, civilising, the “dark continent”. Does this speech inherit that moral geography? Who is imagined to be standing in the dark?
From justice to war
Trace the vocabulary: “bring them to justice” (courts and police), “no distinction between the terrorists… and those who harbor them”, “win the war against terrorism”. Where does the frame change? What does each frame allow a government to do, and can a war against a noun ever end?
